# JDGF Cognitive Interview Guide

**Version:** 1.0 Candidate  
**Status:** Ready for pre-pilot cognitive testing

## 1. Purpose

This guide tests how assessors and institutional respondents understand, retrieve information for, judge, and answer the JDGF assessment items. It is intended to detect ambiguity, hidden assumptions, excessive burden, evidence-access problems, and inconsistent interpretations before an institutional pilot.

Cognitive testing evaluates the response process; it does not validate the scoring model by itself.

## 2. Recommended participants

Recruit participants representing several roles:

- court or institutional leadership;
- registry or records staff;
- judicial statistics staff;
- IT, security, or privacy personnel;
- data owners or stewards;
- internal audit, inspection, or assurance;
- legal, ethics, or human-rights specialists;
- assessors unfamiliar with JDGF.

Where possible, include participants from institutions of different sizes and levels of digitalization.

## 3. Ethics and confidentiality

Before the session:

- explain the research purpose and voluntary nature of participation;
- obtain required institutional and participant authorization;
- avoid collecting unnecessary judicial, personal, classified, or case-level information;
- prohibit disclosure of live credentials, sensitive case facts, or protected evidence;
- explain recording, retention, anonymization, and withdrawal arrangements;
- store notes and recordings securely and dispose of them under the approved plan.

## 4. Session structure

Suggested duration: 45–75 minutes.

1. Introduction and consent — 5–10 minutes;
2. Think-aloud practice — 5 minutes;
3. Assessment items — 25–45 minutes;
4. Scoring-anchor exercise — 10 minutes;
5. Debrief — 10 minutes.

Do not teach the intended answer during testing. Neutral clarification may be provided only after the participant has explained their interpretation.

## 5. Standard introduction

> We are testing the questionnaire, not you or your institution. Please describe what each question means to you, how you would find the answer, what evidence you would use, and what makes the question easy or difficult. There are no correct institutional answers in this exercise. We need to identify wording or scoring rules that could be misunderstood.

## 6. Think-aloud probes

For each selected item, ask:

1. What does this question mean in your own words?
2. Which words or concepts are unclear?
3. What institutional activity or capability is the question asking about?
4. Who would know the answer?
5. What documents, records, logs, observations, or interviews would count as evidence?
6. Is that evidence realistically obtainable?
7. What time period did you assume?
8. Does the question contain more than one requirement?
9. Could two reasonable assessors interpret it differently?
10. Does the item apply to your type of institution? Why or why not?
11. Could an institution answer positively without having an effective capability?
12. What wording would be clearer?

## 7. Scoring-anchor probes

After the participant considers an item, present the 0–5 anchors and ask:

- Which anchor best fits the hypothetical evidence?
- What evidence would be required to move from 1 to 2?
- What formal evidence would justify 3?
- What operating evidence would justify 4?
- What completed improvement cycle would justify 5?
- Is the difference between adjacent levels clear?
- Would another assessor choose a different level?
- Does the lowest-fully-evidenced-anchor rule help or confuse?

## 8. Critical-control probes

For items proposed as critical:

- What harm could result from failure?
- Would the failure justify capping the dimension or overall maturity?
- Is the failure always critical, or only in certain scopes?
- What evidence would demonstrate remediation?
- Could the proposed cap unfairly penalize an institution because responsibility is centralized elsewhere?

## 9. AI-readiness probes

Test explicitly whether participants understand that:

- AI readiness is not the same as AI adoption;
- no current AI use does not automatically produce a low score;
- a mature institution may decide not to use AI;
- readiness includes authority to approve, restrict, suspend, or retire systems;
- data quality, lawful purpose, human oversight, contestability, monitoring, and exit arrangements are prerequisites.

Ask participants to explain these distinctions in their own words.

## 11. Debrief questions

1. Which questions were hardest to understand?
2. Which questions would be hardest to evidence?
3. Which questions seemed repetitive?
4. Which important capability was missing?
5. Which terms require glossary definitions?
6. Did any question appear accusatory or likely to produce defensive answers?
7. Was the 0–5 scale usable?
8. Were confidence ratings understandable?
9. Was the expected workload realistic?
10. What should be changed before field testing?

## 12. Analysis and revision rules

A problem should normally trigger revision when:

- several participants interpret the item differently;
- the intended evidence is unavailable or disproportionate;
- the item combines separable capabilities;
- participants cannot distinguish adjacent anchors;
- the item is systematically answered from aspiration rather than operation;
- applicability differs by institution type without an explicit routing rule;
- sensitive wording creates avoidable disclosure risk;
- the item duplicates another item without analytical value.

All revisions must be entered in `JDGF-Review-and-Revision-Log.md`. Retain the original wording, evidence from testing, decision, rationale, approver, and version affected.

## 13. Minimum completion criteria before institutional pilot

Before the first authorized pilot:

- every assessment item should be tested at least once;
- all critical items should be tested with multiple professional perspectives;
- unresolved critical or high-severity comprehension problems should be corrected;
- scoring anchors should be tested using common hypothetical evidence;
- the instrument should have a documented estimated completion burden;
- the glossary should include all repeatedly misunderstood terms.
