# JDGF Implementation Guide

**Version:** 1.0 Working Draft  
**Status:** Pre-validation

## 1. Purpose

This guide provides a staged approach for adopting and improving judicial data governance using JDGF. It is designed for institutions with different mandates, sizes, digital capabilities, and resource levels.

## 2. Implementation principles

- begin with institutional mission, law, rights, and risk;
- secure accountable leadership before creating technical solutions;
- prioritize critical data and high-impact processes;
- build on existing structures where they are effective;
- separate accountability from technical custody;
- document decisions and retain evidence;
- pursue achievable increments rather than uncontrolled transformation;
- measure outcomes and adapt continuously.

## 3. Implementation phases

### Phase 1 — Mobilize

**Objectives:** establish mandate, sponsorship, scope, and implementation capacity.

**Actions:**

- appoint an executive sponsor and implementation lead;
- define the institutional scope and stakeholders;
- approve a temporary governance mandate;
- identify legal, operational, and transformation drivers;
- establish an implementation team and communication plan;
- identify immediate critical risks.

**Outputs:** mandate, stakeholder map, implementation charter, initial risk register, and work plan.

### Phase 2 — Diagnose

**Objectives:** understand the current state and establish a baseline.

**Actions:**

- inventory institutions, systems, data domains, exchanges, and policies;
- conduct a JDGF baseline assessment;
- identify critical data elements and authoritative sources;
- map responsibilities and decision rights;
- document legal, rights, security, quality, lifecycle, and interoperability gaps.

**Outputs:** baseline report, maturity profile, evidence register, gap analysis, and priority risks.

### Phase 3 — Design

**Objectives:** define the target governance operating model.

**Actions:**

- establish the data governance council;
- assign owners, stewards, custodians, and assurance responsibilities;
- define policies, standards, processes, controls, and indicators;
- set target maturity by dimension;
- design issue-management and escalation mechanisms;
- prepare the prioritized roadmap and resource plan.

**Outputs:** governance charter, role matrix, target operating model, policy roadmap, indicator set, and approved improvement plan.

### Phase 4 — Implement

**Objectives:** operationalize priority capabilities.

**Actions:**

- implement governance forums and decision records;
- create the data inventory, glossary, metadata catalogue, and quality rules;
- formalize access, sharing, retention, publication, and incident controls;
- train leaders, owners, stewards, custodians, and users;
- launch issue-management and performance reporting;
- implement high-priority remediation projects.

**Outputs:** operating controls, trained role holders, governance records, working registers, and completed priority actions.

### Phase 5 — Assure

**Objectives:** verify design and operating effectiveness.

**Actions:**

- review control evidence and performance indicators;
- test selected processes and data samples;
- conduct internal assurance or independent review;
- validate legal, rights, privacy, security, and integrity safeguards;
- report residual risks and corrective actions.

**Outputs:** assurance report, validated findings, residual risk register, and corrective-action plan.

### Phase 6 — Improve and adapt

**Objectives:** sustain governance and respond to institutional change.

**Actions:**

- repeat maturity assessments periodically;
- monitor outcomes and unresolved issues;
- revise policies and standards;
- expand scope to additional domains and institutions;
- incorporate lessons from incidents, audits, reforms, and pilots;
- govern innovation and emerging technologies through controlled experimentation.

**Outputs:** updated maturity profile, revised roadmap, lessons learned, and approved governance changes.

## 4. Prioritization method

Actions should be prioritized using:

- impact on rights and judicial integrity;
- legal and regulatory urgency;
- security, privacy, continuity, and evidentiary risk;
- importance of the affected judicial process;
- number of institutions or users affected;
- feasibility, cost, dependencies, and implementation capacity;
- expected improvement in data reliability and institutional performance.

## 5. Minimum viable governance baseline

Before advanced analytics or AI initiatives, an institution should at minimum have:

- accountable executive sponsorship;
- identified data owners for critical domains;
- a current inventory of critical data and systems;
- basic classifications and access controls;
- approved definitions for critical data elements;
- quality-issue reporting and remediation;
- retention and preservation rules;
- incident, backup, and continuity procedures;
- documented authorization for data sharing and high-risk use.

## 6. Change management

Implementation requires sustained engagement. Institutions should communicate the purpose of governance, involve operational users, address incentives and workload, develop role-specific competencies, and demonstrate early benefits through selected high-value use cases.

## 7. Monitoring

The governance council should review at least:

- maturity improvement;
- critical data risks;
- unresolved quality and access issues;
- control failures and incidents;
- implementation milestones;
- policy exceptions;
- training and role coverage;
- benefits and operational outcomes.

## 8. Sustainability

JDGF should be integrated into institutional planning, budgeting, risk management, internal control, audit, digital transformation, records management, and performance review. Temporary project structures should transition into permanent accountable governance arrangements.
