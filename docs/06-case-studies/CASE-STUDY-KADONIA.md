# JDGF Illustrative Case Study — Mando First-Instance Court, Republic of Kadonia

**Version:** 1.0 Candidate  
**Status:** Fully fictional, desk-based methodological test — not institutional validation  
**Language:** Canonical English version; the original French working case informed this revision

## 1. Purpose and limitations

This case tests whether the consolidated JDGF architecture can be applied coherently to a plausible judicial institution. The Republic of Kadonia, the Mando First-Instance Court, all actors, evidence, scores, and findings are fictional.

The case:

- does not use real judicial data;
- does not establish empirical validity, reliability, feasibility, or institutional impact;
- uses only the facts explicitly defined in the fictional scenario;
- applies the eight JDGF dimensions, six capability attributes, 0–5 scoring anchors, evidence-confidence ratings, and critical-control caps;
- treats all scores as illustrative and provisional.

## 2. Fictional institutional context

The Mando First-Instance Court is a medium-sized fictional court processing approximately 4,000 civil and criminal matters per year.

Its operating context is as follows:

- hearing activity is recorded in paper registers;
- pending-case stock is tracked in a shared spreadsheet without individual password-based access;
- the Ministry of Justice has a judicial-statistics service;
- aggregated court data are transmitted twice yearly by email;
- there is no binding common classification or nomenclature across courts;
- data are manually re-entered at central level;
- local quality checks are informal and led by the chief registrar;
- institutional responsibilities are understood in practice but are not formally documented;
- a national justice-digitalisation initiative has begun;
- no artificial-intelligence project is currently deployed or formally proposed.

## 3. Functional governance roles

| JDGF functional role | Fictional assignment | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Data producer | Court registry officers | Operating informally |
| Data steward | Chief registrar | Informal assignment |
| Data owner | Head of court | Accountability not documented |
| Technical custodian | Regional IT focal point | Limited and remote support |
| Governance authority | Ministry judicial-statistics service by default | Mandate mismatch; no dedicated governance body |
| Authorized user | Relevant ministry directorate | Use conditions not formally documented |

The principal governance weakness is not the absence of people performing work. It is the absence of formally approved mandates, decision rights, evidence requirements, escalation routes, and assurance mechanisms.

## 5. Scoring method

Each dimension is assessed across six recurring capability attributes:

1. mandate and policy;
2. roles and accountability;
3. processes and controls;
4. information and evidence;
5. measurement and assurance;
6. improvement and adaptation.

Scores use the lowest fully evidenced anchor. Missing evidence is scored as absence or weakness; it is not treated as Not Applicable.

The attribute mean is calculated for transparency, but gates and caps override compensatory averaging.

## 8. Gate and cap analysis

The institution cannot claim Level 3 in any dimension because it lacks approved policies, formally assigned accountability, and repeatable evidenced governance processes.

The identified security weakness is a potentially critical control failure. In a real assessment, assessors would determine whether the spreadsheet contains personal, sensitive, evidentiary, or operationally critical data and whether unauthorized access has occurred. If classified as critical, the security dimension and overall maturity would be capped at Level 2 until remediation is verified. The raw profile remains below that cap.

No score above Level 2 is supportable because:

- no approved governance instruments are available;
- no representative operating-effectiveness evidence exists;
- no periodic assurance or completed improvement cycle is evidenced;
- confidence is low or moderate across all dimensions.

## 9. Priority improvement roadmap

### Priority 1 — Immediate protection and containment

1. identify the data contained in the shared spreadsheet;
2. restrict access to named users;
3. establish individual authentication and a least-privilege access list;
4. create a protected backup and test restoration;
5. document the incident and escalation process;
6. migrate sensitive tracking to an approved controlled environment.

### Priority 2 — Establish minimum institutional governance

1. approve a proportionate judicial data-governance mandate;
2. appoint accountable data owners and operational stewards;
3. designate an existing body or function as governance authority without creating a mandatory new office;
4. approve a decision-rights and escalation matrix;
5. create a basic evidence and issue register.

### Priority 3 — Stabilize definitions, quality, and exchanges

1. inventory critical data elements and reporting flows;
2. approve a minimum common nomenclature;
3. document field definitions and validation rules;
4. eliminate avoidable manual re-entry;
5. record data-quality issues and corrective actions;
6. introduce completeness, timeliness, and revision indicators.

### Priority 4 — Govern records and rights

1. identify applicable legal, privacy, procedural, archival, and evidentiary requirements;
2. define retention and preservation responsibilities;
3. document access, disclosure, correction, complaint, and redress routes;
4. protect authenticity and traceability of critical records;
5. approve lawful disposition and archival-transfer procedures.

### Priority 5 — Build decision-ready and AI-ready governance

1. document statistical production and release controls;
2. define approved secondary uses and disclosure-risk review;
3. establish an inventory and approval process for analytics and future AI proposals;
4. define prohibited or high-risk AI uses;
5. require impact assessment, human oversight, traceability, and exit controls before procurement or deployment;
6. recognize that a documented decision not to deploy AI may be a mature outcome.

## 10. Methodological observations

This revised case exposes several instrument requirements:

- every item needs a stable identifier;
- design evidence and operating evidence must be recorded separately;
- adverse evidence must be captured, not merely missing evidence;
- assessors need guidance for scoring ordinary administrative activity that is not yet governed;
- confidence must be assigned independently of maturity;
- AI non-use must not be treated as automatic immaturity;
- critical-control classification requires a documented decision and cannot be inferred solely from the arithmetic score;
- the instrument should show raw scores, gates, caps, and moderated results separately.

## 11. Conclusion

Kadonia demonstrates that the consolidated JDGF can distinguish between:

- work that is being performed;
- governance capability that is formally established;
- evidence that the capability operates;
- confidence in the assessment;
- and critical weaknesses that arithmetic averaging must not conceal.

The case remains a fictional methodological test. It supports instrument refinement, not empirical validation.
